The upstairs and the basement were rebuilt from nothing. Before, each was five or three rooms stretched into tubes twenty tiles deep, hanging off one corridor and one staircase. Open the Map to see all three floors.

Upstairs: the private floor

  • Smaller than the ground floor and set in from the sides, with the roof below showing round it.
  • One gallery runs east to west with every door on it, so standing there shows you who goes where.
  • A staircase at each end: the old back stairs, and new front stairs up from the foyer.
  • Rooms pair up through connecting doors, so the gallery is never the only way round.
  • New rooms: a sewing room, a sunroom, and a bathroom of your own.
  • Your bedroom is the one deliberate dead end: a single door to bolt, with the cameras behind it.

The basement: backstage

  • An L of service rooms that does not reach under the front of the house.
  • New service stairs drop from the kitchen into a cold store. The cellar stairs still drop from the back landing.
  • The rec room is the hub, with a door in each wall.
  • New rooms: cold store, laundry, wine cellar.
  • The hidden room now has two doors, one from the cold store and one from the wine cellar.
  • The walkout to the back lawn is where it was.

What guided it

  • Loops, not dead ends. Hitman and Dishonored designers say real floor plans are full of dead ends and games need loops, so nobody is ever trapped and there is always a second route.
  • Public to private. Victorian houses separate social rooms, private rooms and service rooms. Here that is ground, upstairs and basement.
  • A crowd with a backstage round it. The party is the public layer; the basement connects behind it.
  • Rooms sized for what is in them. Every room has one furniture group and clear paths between its doors.

Second pass: art, light and motion

Every character, piece of furniture, fixture, snack and emote was redrawn, and how people move and act was rebuilt. Press Art in the demo to see each old sprite beside its replacement.

Characters

  • One shared rig, three real views: toward you, away, and in profile. Before, walking sideways slid a front-facing sprite.
  • A four-frame walk in every view, timed to distance covered so feet do not slide.
  • New actions: reach, talk, shove, stumble, carry a plate, dance, wave goodbye.
  • Turning shows in posture as well as colour: slouch, then hunch, then arms out and lit eyes.
  • Hair, outfits and colours still come from your data, so nobody's identity changed.

Furniture and light

  • 29 props redrawn with a top face and a front face, so a sofa reads as a sofa.
  • Lamps, fridge, speaker and surveillance desk look different when on. Candles, televisions, string lights and the disco ball animate.
  • Light stops at walls and spills through doorways. Before, a lamp lit the next room through the wall.
  • Lit parts glow over the night: shades, screens, flames, the open fridge.
  • Real doors that swing open as someone passes, and show a bolt when locked.

Movement and interaction

  • The host walks over and performs each job. The lamp comes on when the hand reaches it, not when you click.
  • A shoved guest stumbles back with a puff of dust. A talked-to guest answers.
  • Guests walk by condition: tipsy ones weave, mutating ones drag, feral ones scuttle.
  • Idle guests look around. Whoever is speaking gestures.

MethodRamps shift hue as they darken (shadows toward violet, highlights toward yellow), light always comes from the upper left, and outlines take the colour of what they enclose. For walk mechanics I studied the open-licensed Liberated Pixel Cup walk sheets, the one reference set this workspace could actually download and look at; nothing was copied from it. Other technique notes came from search summaries of Pixelblog on colour ramps, top-down character sprites and top-down objects.

Not doneRugs and floor textures were left as they were. The sprites are drawn by code in redesign/src/art; they are not yet exported into the game's own sprite files.

Why it reads as an app today

12%of the screen is the house at the default view
16 pxheight of a guest at that view
6 + 8text panels and top-bar controls always on screen
3fixed zoom steps, none of which follow you
  • The world is a thumbnail. The play area is a window inside a dashboard, and the house is a smaller picture inside that window. At 16 pixels tall you cannot read a face, a mood or how far someone has turned, so the game has to tell you in a sidebar instead.
  • Everything is written, nothing is shown. Time, suspicion, your plan and every guest's state are sentences in panels. The panels use small-caps serif type, which is how websites look, and the art is pixel art. Two styles on one screen.
  • The walls are flat. The house is drawn from directly above, like a floor plan, with walls as black bands. The furniture and people are drawn from the front. The two views disagree, so rooms look like diagrams.
  • Every room has the same floor. The same brown brick runs through the lounge, hall, study and ballroom, so you tell rooms apart by reading nameplates.
  • Nothing exists outside the walls. The neighbours are the reason suspicion exists, and they are not on screen. The lawn is one flat green.
  • You operate it like a form. Pick a floor from a list, pick a room from a dropdown, click the tile next to you, choose from a menu. Walking across a room is many separate clicks.

What changed in the demo

The map

  • The house fills the screen. On a laptop a tile is 48 px and a person stands about 80 px tall.
  • The camera follows you. Zoom out twice to see the whole lot.
  • North walls have a face with wallpaper and panelling. Each room has its own paper and floor.
  • A street, four neighbours, streetlamps, a fence, flower beds, and party litter on the front lawn.
  • Rooms are lit by default. Lamps and candles add warm pools. Outdoors is night.

The interface

  • Four corners: guests, clock and street, tools, End Hour.
  • The clock is the budget. Plan 23 minutes and it reads 12:23.
  • Guests are faces. The border colour is how far gone they are, the bar is agitation.
  • What people say arrives in a dialogue box with their portrait.
  • One map for all three floors replaces the floor list and the room dropdown.
  • A title screen and two endings.
  • Works on a phone.

How it plays

  • Click anywhere to walk there. The route and its cost show first.
  • Pick a verb, then a target. You walk over and do it in one click.
  • One undo takes back one whole click.
  • End Hour plays out: the screen letterboxes, guests walk, the hour is announced.
  • When a new window lights, the camera glances at the street.

Gameplay problems I found while playing

The demo does not fix these, because they change your rules and that is your decision.

  • BiggestGuests take one step an hour. You take up to sixty. The house is 56 tiles wide and everyone is Feral by hour 18, so leading one guest from the ballroom to the street takes longer than the whole night. This is also why an hour feels like nothing happened. Letting guests walk four to six tiles an hour would make every End Hour visibly move the board.
  • The real game has no ending. Suspicion stops at 100 and nothing happens. The demo adds simple win and lose screens to show the shape.
  • Five of twelve guests cannot be lured outside. Every lamp and speaker is fixed indoors, so light-lovers and bass-lovers have nothing to follow to the door. Your own project report notes this. A carried lamp and a portable speaker would use the toolbar slots well.
  • Suspicion only rises. There is no reward for a quiet hour or for getting someone home.
  • Which way a push goes depends on where you stand, and the current game never shows it. The demo previews it.
